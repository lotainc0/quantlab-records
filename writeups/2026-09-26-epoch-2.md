# Three pre-registered studies on daily bars: two negatives, one narrow positive

**Date:** 2026-09-26
**Author:** Yuepheng Yang
**Status:** final for these three studies. Corrections, if any, go to
[`ERRATA.md`](../ERRATA.md), never into this file silently.

The first writeup closed the 5-minute arena
([three measurements](2026-09-19-three-measurements.md)); the second found that
no walk-forward had ever been compared with simply holding the index
([what buy-and-hold was doing](2026-09-20-what-buy-and-hold-was-doing.md)).
This one covers Epoch 2: daily bars over up to a century, three studies, 17
counted trials, each with its bar fixed before its one look at the data.

---

## The short version

| study | question | result |
|---|---|---|
| **Trend-following**, 11 sector and broad ETFs, 2001–2026 | does it beat holding SPY? | **CLOSED.** Out-of-sample Sharpe 0.279 against SPY's 0.569 |
| **Sector rotation**, the nine sector SPDRs, 2002–2026 | does ranking sectors by momentum beat SPY? | **CLOSED.** Sharpe 0.537 against SPY's 0.596; holding all nine equally earned 0.618 |
| **Industry momentum after publication**, Ken French's 48 industries | did the published effect survive its own publication? | **SURVIVED, narrowly.** +0.24% a month over equal weight after 1999, t 1.90 against a bar of 1.645. Not investable |

On everything I tested that could actually be traded, the answer was to hold
the market, or the sectors equally. The one effect that persisted lives in
research portfolios nobody can buy, and it clears its bar by a margin that a
stricter test would erase. Both statements are the result.

---

## The rules every study ran under

- **Pre-registration.** The hypothesis, universe, dates, parameter grid, trial
  count, bar and reading rule are written down and committed before the run.
  Any change afterwards is a dated amendment with a reason, made before the
  look, or it is an erratum.
- **An honest trial count.** Every configuration tried is a trial, and the
  bar charges for all of them (the deflated Sharpe ratio, Bailey & López de
  Prado 2014). A second look at the same grid is more trials, not a do-over.
- **A benchmark on every result.** Beating noise is not enough; the result has
  to beat what doing nothing would have earned over the same stretch.
- **One look.** Each study is run once. Instrument checks, decided in advance,
  are the only grounds for voiding a run. A result I dislike is not one of
  them.
- **Frozen data.** The ETF bars and the French industry file are committed and
  pinned by hash, so the same command gives the same answer anywhere.
- **Independent review.** Before the sector study's look, a separate model
  reviewed the instrument and found a real defect (below); a second, fresh
  reader then checked the repair. The industry study had no independent review
  before its look, only its own tests. That is a gap, and it is stated here
  rather than discovered later.

## Study 1 — trend-following on ETFs

**Prior.** Trend-following has worked for a long time on paper: positive in
each of 67 markets from 1880 to 2016, average Sharpe about 0.4 (Hurst, Ooi &
Pedersen 2017). The investable evidence is thinner: trend-timing 31 industry
ETFs after commissions earned a Sharpe of 0.61 against SPY's 0.59 over
2005–2024 (Zarattini & Antonacci, Table 7).

**Bar.** Four pre-registered configurations, declared as **12 trials** because
the grid was looked at three times, each look following an instrument repair.
Out-of-sample Sharpe above 0.66 at that count, above SPY's, and positive alpha.

**Result** (`wf-sectors-daily-carry`, 11 ETFs, 6,411 out-of-sample days):

| | measured | required |
|---|---|---|
| out-of-sample Sharpe | **0.279** | 0.66, and above SPY's 0.569 |
| alpha | +0.27% a year | above 0 |
| total return | +42.1% | SPY: +889.2% |

**CLOSED**, one condition of three. The three repairs before it each raised
the number (0.080, then 0.119, then 0.279) and none changed the verdict. The
repairs were: daily bars served with unadjusted splits, a cost model 12.6× too
high at daily spacing, and a walk-forward that sold its whole book at every
fold boundary.

**Found later, and not re-run.** The walk-forward chose each later fold's
parameters on the cash left beside the carried positions, 21% to 100% of the
fold's equity, not on the equity itself. The selection was probably little
changed at those sizes, but that is an inference. The grid is closed after
three looks, so it is recorded in the errata rather than run a fourth time.

## Study 2 — sector rotation (cross-sectional momentum)

**Prior**, re-read from the primary tables after the run. That correction is
dated in the spec:
- Faber (2010): holding the top 3 of 10 sectors by 12-month return earned a
  Sharpe of 0.55 against 0.35 for equal weight, 1928–2009, gross.
- The modern, investable sample is Beluska (Quantpedia 2024): the same nine
  SPDRs, 1998–2024, gross. Top 3 earned 0.55, the equal-weight nine 0.57, the
  S&P 500 0.49.
- So the honest expectation was *no better than holding the sectors*.

**Bar.** Four configurations, **4 trials**:
- a deflated-Sharpe probability of at least 0.95, which at the realised skew
  and kurtosis means a Sharpe of about 0.547–0.551;
- a Sharpe above SPY's;
- positive alpha.

A pass inside the noise was to be reported as indistinguishable from SPY.

**Before the look.**
- Four engine changes were built and proven not to move any existing result.
- An independent pre-run review found that later folds chose parameters on a
  near-empty book (the defect from Study 1, far worse for a fully invested
  strategy). It was repaired, and seven dated amendments were made, all before
  the run.
- The report cannot compute its descriptive curves unless it first reproduces
  the headline run to within a millionth of a dollar.

**Result** (`wf-sectors9-xsmom`, 6,194 out-of-sample days, every instrument
check passed):

| | measured | required |
|---|---|---|
| deflated-Sharpe probability | **0.946** | ≥ 0.95 |
| Sharpe | **0.537** | above SPY's **0.596** |
| Sharpe difference vs SPY | −0.059 ± 0.091 | — |
| alpha | +0.02% a year (t 0.01) | above 0 |

**CLOSED**, two conditions of three failed. It is market exposure at a beta
of 0.80, not an edge.

*Exploratory, not part of the verdict:*
- Holding all nine sectors equally, rebalanced monthly, earned **0.618**,
  above both the strategy and SPY. The ranking subtracted value.
- The four configurations run individually: 0.418, 0.569, 0.498, 0.586. Two of
  them clear the probability bar on their own, but none beats SPY. Choosing the
  best of four after seeing them is the multiple testing the bar exists to
  charge for, so this is not a near-pass.
- **Scope:** this closes sector rotation on these nine funds. It does not
  close momentum as a family.

## Study 3 — industry momentum after its publication

**Why this study.**
- **Breadth.** With the market's common move removed, the nine sectors carry
  about 7 independent bets and the 48 industries about 25.
- **Not investable.** The French industry portfolios cannot be bought, so the
  honest question was not "is there an edge I can trade" but "did a published
  effect survive being published".

**Prior.** Moskowitz & Grinblatt (1999) found that holding the three
strongest of 20 industries beat the middle three by 0.36% a month, t 4.38,
over 1963–1995 (their Table III, read from the page). McLean & Pontiff (2016)
find published predictors earn 58% less after publication; applied here, that
predicts about 0.15% a month, almost exactly this study's bar.

**Rule and bar.** Their headline rule, taken from the paper, not chosen on our
data, so **1 trial**:
- rank the 48 industries on 6-month return;
- hold the top 7 at equal weight for 6 months, in overlapping cohorts;
- measure the monthly return against the equal-weighted 48.

It must first **replicate** the paper in the paper's own years, which is the
check that the instrument works. Only then is the post-publication **test**
read.

**Result** (`imom-decay`, every instrument check passed):

| window | mean over equal weight | t | gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| replication, 1963-07 → 1995-07 | +0.295% a month | 2.93 | t ≥ 2: **pass** |
| test, 1999-01 → 2026-08 | **+0.241% a month** | **1.90** | t ≥ 1.645: **pass** |

**SURVIVED**, by the rule written before the run. How narrow that is, stated
beside the verdict, not after it:
- t 1.90 would fail an ordinary two-sided 5% test (1.96).
- By decade, the post-publication edge is the 2000s +0.12% (t 0.48), the 2010s
  +0.10% (t 0.92), the 2020s +0.50% (t 2.24). Much of the evidence is the last
  six years.
- It is gross of costs. A one-way trading cost of 0.93% would erase it, at
  about 156% turnover a year. These portfolios have no fund you could trade
  them through.

It is an effect, not an edge, and it earns no claim until it replicates
somewhere investable.

## The family: 17 trials, counted together

Each study was judged against its own pre-registered bar. As **added
disclosure, not a changed bar**, here is what the three headlines look like
treated as one family:

| | one-sided p | Holm threshold | BHY threshold |
|---|---|---|---|
| industry momentum (t 1.90) | 0.029 | 0.017 | 0.009 |
| sector rotation (deflated) | 0.054 | 0.025 | 0.018 |
| trend-following (deflated) | 0.60 | 0.050 | 0.027 |

**None survives either correction.** The p-values test different nulls: the
Sharpe studies test "better than zero after deflation", the industry study
"better than equal weight". The table is a disclosure, not a combined test.

The same point in terms of trials:
- Counted as one search across all 17 trials, the industry result would need
  t ≥ 2.75 (Bonferroni, one-sided).
- The two Sharpe studies together spent 16 trials. At 16 trials over 6,194
  days the deflated-Sharpe bar is 0.695. Neither came close.

The 5-minute epoch is a separate record, reported in its own writeup, and its
runs are never pooled with these.

## What went wrong, and how it was caught

Each item links to its dated entry in [`ERRATA.md`](../ERRATA.md) or
[`EPOCH.md`](../EPOCH.md).

- **Walk-forward folds chose parameters on leftover cash, not equity.** Found
  by an independent pre-run review; repaired before the sector study; recorded
  against the trend result, which was not re-run.
- **Symbol order changed results.** The same bars in a different order gave
  243 trades instead of 239. The ledger records order only through the preset
  name.
- **The out-of-sample windows began with dead time.** 21.7% of the trend
  study's out-of-sample bars could not make a decision while the strategy
  warmed up. Disclosed; new runs align the warmup.
- **The sector study's prior was mis-cited.** It said "Sharpe 0.40–0.50", and
  one drawdown figure appears in no table. Corrected from the primary tables,
  after the run, dated.
- **The breadth argument used the wrong measure.** It said "2.27 effective
  bets", which is total diversification. The right measure for a ranking is
  relative breadth, about 7. Corrected after the run.
- **No walk-forward had ever been compared with buy-and-hold.** The previous
  writeup.
- **At the one look, the pre-declared check script failed to run** (a shell
  quoting bug). No check had executed; it was fixed and every check passed.
- **The industry calculator's own test caught a library defect before the
  run.** pandas 3 silently reordered months, which would have measured
  turnover between the wrong months.
- **A paper-trading account number was public for a week.** My search for it
  was broken, and at first I said it had never been published. It is now
  redacted from the current record; the history is not rewritten.

The pattern matters more than any single item. **Every one was found by a
second, independent measurement or reader**, never by re-reading my own work.
That is why the studies above carry reproducers, cross-checks and pre-declared
checks, and why the one study without an independent pre-run review says so.

## What would change these conclusions

- **Trend-following.** A different parameter selection in the folds affected
  by the cash defect. This is inferred to be small and not re-run. The gap to
  SPY (0.279 against 0.569) is large.
- **Sector rotation.** Little would. It fails two of three conditions, its
  best single configuration (0.586) is still below SPY, and holding the sectors
  equally beat it.
- **Industry momentum.** Easily. At t 1.90 against 1.645, a few bad years in
  the 2020s would flip it, and so would realistic trading costs. It is the
  result to be most skeptical of, and the only one worth testing again,
  somewhere investable.

**Not tested, by decision:**
- the cost model, which reconciliation showed is conservative;
- the 5-minute arena, closed on evidence;
- news sentiment, measured to hurt;
- leverage;
- any further long-only market-timing strategy (that family has failed
  repeatedly here);
- single stocks. The free point-in-time data is missing prices for about half
  of the stocks that have left the index and has no delisting returns, so that
  question starts with a feasibility check, not a study.
